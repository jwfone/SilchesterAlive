# The public baths: how this reconstruction was made

Nothing of Calleva's public baths stands above ground today; the site is a field. What you see is a
reconstruction: the plan is surveyed and excavated fact, while most of what rises above about a metre
is an informed reconstruction. The colours in the **Evidence** view show which is which.

## The date shown

The baths were in use for some 350 years and were rebuilt and altered many times. This model shows them
as they may have looked **around AD 270–280**, soon after the town's stone wall was built, the same date as
the rest of the town in this reconstruction.

- Changes known to be later are left out. A new heated room on the east side was built after AD 388
  (a coin of that date was found beneath it). The caldarium's eastern apse is shown on the 1905 plan as
  part of the same, latest phase of work, so it is left out too.
- Where a change could be either late 3rd or 4th century (the rectangular rebuild of the latrine and the
  enlargement of the west warm room), the earlier form is shown.

## How the plan was made

- **1903–4:** the Society of Antiquaries excavated the baths and in 1905 published a plan showing every room
  and six phases of building (Hope and Fox).
- **GIS:** the Silchester Mapping Project digitised the Antiquaries' "Great Plan" of the whole town. The 1905
  baths plan was matched to those digitised walls to within about 20 cm, so the model sits on the surveyed
  wall lines.
- **2018–21:** the University of Reading re-excavated parts of the baths. They showed that the first
  civic baths (c. AD 55–65, under Nero) were almost entirely demolished, and a larger bath-house was built
  around AD 85 on the same alignment. That Flavian building is, with later alterations, the plan published
  in 1905. They also found a small, possibly even earlier, brick building on the east side that stood until
  the 4th century.
- The building follows the line of the Iron Age town ditch rather than the Roman street grid, which is why it
  sits slightly askew to the street.

## Walking through the building (north to south)

1. **Street front:** a colonnade of small limestone columns on a low brick base, with the entrance between
   two brick piers about 3.2 m apart. The latrine fills the east end of the frontage.
2. **Palaestra:** an open exercise court surrounded by a covered walk, its roof carried on columns standing
   on a low wall. The walk was floored with white chalk mosaic.
3. **Apodyterium:** the changing hall, the full width of the building.
4. **Frigidarium:** the cold room, with a stone basin (labrum) for washing, and a **cold plunge bath** to
   the west.
5. **Tepidaria:** warm rooms over underfloor heating (hypocausts), with a stoke hole on the west.
6. **Caldarium:** the hot room, with an apse at its west end; beyond it a **second hot room** with its own
   apse, heated from the **furnace (praefurnium)** projecting at the south end.

## What is evidence and what is reconstruction

**Green: found at Silchester.** The wall lines and room plan; the wall build of small squared Greensand blocks
in level courses with every joint broken, and bands of three courses of long thin red bricks (proportions taken
from the excavation photographs); small limestone columns (one drum is 0.25 m across); the brick entrance piers; wedge-shaped
and hollow arch bricks and wall-heating tiles, which show that the hot rooms were vaulted; roof tiles; pieces
of large glass window panes found west of the cold bath; pink painted wall plaster; mosaic and pink mortar floors.

**Amber: by comparison with buildings that still stand, and Roman building rules.** Wall heights follow
standing Roman bath walls in Britain: the "Old Work" at Wroxeter (about 7 m), the Jewry Wall at Leicester
(about 9 m) and the bath-house at Ravenglass (about 4 m). Window sizes follow a glazed window found in a
bath-house apse at Great Chesters on Hadrian's Wall (about 1.5 × 1.2 m). The Roman architect Vitruvius
says hot rooms should take light from the south-west, a window should light the washing basin, and
vaults are better than timber ceilings. Column heights use the classical rule of about seven times the
column's width.

**Red: conjecture.** The upper parts of the latrine, furnace and early brick building; the height of the
palaestra walls; the pitch of the roofs; and most doorways between rooms (the 1905 plan records floors, not
thresholds).

## Choices worth knowing about

- The **frigidarium** has a timber roof rather than a vault, so a high window can light the washing basin.
- **Pools** are shown as basins with a raised rim rather than sunk into the floor.
- The **entrance** is shown with a brick arch over the two piers; only the piers survive.
- The outside walls are shown as bare stone and brick. They may have been rendered (plastered) outside;
  there is no evidence either way.
