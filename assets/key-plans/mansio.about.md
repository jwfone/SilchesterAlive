# The mansio: how this reconstruction was made

Nothing of this building stands above ground today. It was dug in the 1870s, planned by the Ordnance Survey and covered
over again about 1880; the Society of Antiquaries added to the plan in 1893. What you see is a reconstruction. The plan is
surveyed fact: every wall sits on the Society of Antiquaries' "Great Plan", as digitised by the Silchester Mapping Project.
Almost everything above about a metre is an informed reconstruction. The colours in the **Evidence** view show which is which.

## What the building was

The second-largest building in Calleva, in Insula VIII near the south gate. Its excavators thought it a cavalry barracks
(Joyce, in the 1870s), then a *hospitium*, a public guest-house (1893). It is now usually called a **mansio**: an official inn
for people travelling on state business by the imperial post (*cursus publicus*), with many plain rooms for travellers, twin baths, a large
latrine and a granary, close to the town wall for easy access to paddocks. Its closest parallels are at Caerwent and Chelmsford, and Kempten and Heddernheim in Germany (Creighton and Fry); the nearest is
one seen from the air at Wanborough, 30 miles west (Fulford). No inscription names it, so its purpose is a reading of its plan.

## The date shown

Nothing dates the building. From its alignment with the street grid it can hardly be earlier than the mid-2nd century, and
may be much later (Boon). It is shown **around AD 270–280**, the same date as the rest of the town, **as excavated**,
including an alteration of unknown date on the west side.

## Walking through the building

1. **The gate:** near the west end of the north side, a 6 m opening in the outer wall. The excavators said the entrance
   "very much resembled the main entrance to the forum". Through it, across the outer corridor, an **entrance hall** cuts
   through the north wing to the courtyard.
2. **The courtyard:** about 45 × 35 m, almost as big as the forum's open area. Three wings line it; on the east it is closed
   by a wall only.
3. **The wings:** each is a row of rooms between two corridors, one on the courtyard and one on the outside. In the north and
   south wings the rooms are much alike, some divided by a cross wall, as if grouped in sets: rooms for travellers.
4. **The west wing** is wider. It held three rooms with underfloor heating (one large hall), **a stair to an upper floor**, and
   at the north-west corner probably the **kitchen**. An apsidal room and two rooms to its south were added later.
5. **The latrines** were at the east end of the south wing's inner corridor.
6. **The outer court**, east of the courtyard, was found empty: perhaps a garden. In its south-east corner are **the baths**:
   changing room, warm and hot rooms with underfloor heating, a latrine, and a **spring-fed reservoir** walled in brick. Some
   rooms are duplicated, perhaps so that men and women could bathe separately. The drain ran out through the town wall.
7. **The granary**, to the west in its own walled yard, has a buttressed west wall: buttresses held walls against the outward
   push of stored grain.

## What is evidence and what is reconstruction

**Green: found at Silchester.** The wall lines, room plan and thicknesses; the gate; the heated rooms; walls of flint with
brick corners; the bath rooms, their hypocausts, the reservoir and the drains; the granary's buttresses; the red tile floor
of the baths' changing room; a few doorways seen in the 1880s (in the baths) or as gaps in the survey.

**Amber: by comparison.** The courses of tile running through the flint walls (as in the town wall and the forum; none was
recorded here); room heights checked against Vitruvius's rule (half the sum of a room's length and width); tiled roofs at a
low pitch; vaults over the hot rooms; glazed windows, as found at the town's public baths.

**Red: conjecture.** Every height and roof; the upper floor; the windows; most doorways (the survey shows walls, not doors); the
doors and gateway into the outer court; the reservoir's superstructure; the floors (red tile in the corridors, plain mortar in
the rooms, tile in the granary) and the plaster inside.

Note that the wall colours follow the wall plan, so the walls show green even where their facing is borrowed from elsewhere.

## Choices worth knowing about

- **Two storeys only in the west wing.** The one clue to an upper floor is the stair the excavators recorded there. The west
  wing is also the widest and held the best rooms and the kitchen, so it is shown with an upper floor; the other wings are one
  storey. How much of the west wing was two storeys is not known.
- **Heights.** Each room lies between two corridors, so it has no outside wall: it can only be lit by small windows above the
  corridor roofs. That, with corridor walls high enough for a door, gives walls of about 5.8 m, which matches Vitruvius's rule
  for rooms of this size. The two-storey west wing rises to 8 m.
- **The corridors are walled** on the courtyard side, as the survey shows, with windows; they may instead have been open
  porticos.
- **The entrance is not made grand.** The plan gives its width; nothing gives its height.
- **The outer court's door and street gateway are conjecture:** none was recorded, but the court must have been reached
  somehow.
- **A solid block** of masonry in the north wing (in the Great Plan, though the 1893 plan shows a room) is shown as a low
  platform.
- **A later latrine** on the south side, shown dotted on modern plans, is not modelled: it was never surveyed.
- **The walls are flint** with brick corners, as the 1880s records say. The double courses of tile through them are borrowed from
  the town wall and the forum: the mansio's own walls were described only as "composed of flints".
- **The baths are simplified.** Their walls were recorded as mixed flint and stone (grey grits, oolite, even chalk) with one or
  two courses of brick at uneven intervals, and many partitions of brick. They are shown in the same flint as the rest.
- **Floors.** Almost none were recorded. The rooms are given plain mortar floors, the way the excavators read floorless rooms
  nearby ("possibly it was of cement, which has perished"); the corridors red tile, like the one tiled floor found in the baths.
  Joyce found part of the building paved with "very rough, and great heavy flints", as if for stables, but not where, so it
  is not shown.
