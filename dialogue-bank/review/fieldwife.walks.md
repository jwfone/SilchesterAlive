# fieldwife walks

### Walk 1

**Ghost:** Welcome, traveller. My husband is out ploughing our strip fields, and I must feed the hens soon. Have you come to see our little church by the gate?
**You:** Where do you go to pray?
**Ghost:** St Mary's is our little stone church, located near the edge of the ancient walls. It is the heart of our parish, where we gather with our neighbours to worship. The sound of its bell calls us from the fields when it is time to pray.
**You:** Who tells you stories about the King and far places?
**Ghost:** Carters and drovers halting near the east gate often share tidings from distant towns. They speak of King Henry's court, changing grain prices in Winchester, or heavy rains up north. We listen eagerly while they rest their beasts, gathering whatever news passes along the track before returning to our quiet fields.
**You:** How do you pay for things without coins?
**Ghost:** Silver pennies are rare things for simple folk like us, so we barter instead. We trade a basket of beans or fresh eggs for salt and iron nails. Most days pass without a single coin changing hands in our cottage, as we simply share what we have with our neighbours.
**You:** How far do you walk for market?
**Ghost:** Market day means an early walk along the road to the nearby market town. I carry a woven basket filled with fresh hen eggs or surplus broad beans to trade. It is where we acquire salt for preserving meat and iron tools for field work.
**You:** What do you usually eat for dinner?
**Ghost:** Barley bread and warm bean stew are what filling our bowls every day. We keep hens behind the cottage for fresh eggs, which give us good nourishment. Meat is quite scarce, so a broth with garden herbs and dried peas usually sustains our family through the working season.
**You:** How is your barley bread baked?
**Ghost:** Good tide to you. Mind the mud in the furrows as you go.

### Walk 2

**Ghost:** Welcome back, friend! You find me mending a coat before the children return from the fields. It is good to see you again.
**You:** What is your house like?
**Ghost:** Our little cottage is made from flint stones and old tiles we found nearby. It stands near the churchyard, giving us shelter from the weather. We share the single room, keeping a small peat fire burning to cook our daily meals.
**You:** Where do you get tiles to repair your home?
**Ghost:** Fallen red tiles lie buried beneath nettles near the old wall ruins. We carry these heavy blocks back to mend our chimney and line the cottage roof. Gathering stone from the ancient grounds helps all the tenant families keep their hearths sturdy without spending silver pennies.
**You:** Is it hard to dig old red tiles out of weeds?
**Ghost:** Thick vegetation hides old clay tiles lying along the base of the stone walls. My husband uses a wooden spade to unearth flat pieces from under moss and leaf mold. Gathering these scattered stones allows us to repair broken chimney walls without spending precious silver.
**You:** What is your daily routine?
**Ghost:** We rise with the first light of the sun to start our daily tasks. I tend the cooking pot while the children gather dry sticks for the fire. Then we all head out to the strip fields to pull up wild weeds.
**You:** Where do you fetch your water from?
**Ghost:** Fresh water comes from the deep stone well near the manor farm. I carry two heavy wooden pails balanced on a wooden yoke over my shoulders each morning. It is tiring work before dawn, but clean water is needed for our cooking pot, washing, and brewing.
**You:** How do your children help around the farm?
**Ghost:** Good tide to you. Mind the mud in the furrows as you go.

**Ghost:** Farewell! May St Mary keep you safe on your journey today.

### Walk 6

**Ghost:** My husband and I tend strips of land where we sow barley, oats, and broad beans. These long, narrow plots lie inside the giant stone walls of the old town. We work the soil by hand to ensure we have enough grain for winter.
**You:** What tools do you use in the fields?
**Ghost:** We harvest our corn using iron sickles, sweeping the curved blades close to the roots. Heavy wooden spades turn the soil in spring, while iron-tipped hoes keep weeds from choking the barley. Simple gear serves us well enough to put bread upon our table.
**You:** What is a flail used for?
**Ghost:** Flails are made of two heavy wooden sticks joined by a tough leather thong. We beat the dried stalks upon the barn floor until the grain breaks free from the husks. After that, tossing the mixture into the wind blows away the light chaff.
**You:** Tell me about your iron sickles.
**Ghost:** Each curved blade is forged by the village blacksmith from dark iron. I hold a handful of barley stalks in my left palm and slice through them with the sharp edge in my right. It is tiring work, but a good sickle makes short work of the crop.
**You:** How busy is the autumn harvest?
**Ghost:** Harvest is a frantic season for every tenant inside the old walls. We cut the grain, bind it tightly into sheaves, and carry it away to dry. Once the fields are clear, we give hearty thanks at St Mary's church for our daily bread.
**You:** What do you do in different seasons?
**Ghost:** Good tide to you. Mind the mud in the furrows as you go.

**Ghost:** Folk from around the village often scavenge fallen tiles and flint from the crumbling old ramparts. Finding these old materials saves us from buying costly timber or fresh thatch. Patching our cottage walls with gathered red tile keeps the damp air out during the cold, wet months.
**You:** What do you repair with stones gathered from walls?
**Ghost:** Mending broken wall sections with old tile and flint keeps cold drafts from blowing inside. We set flat tile pieces around our cooking fire to create a clean, safe hearth surface. Their heavy weight keeps our roof edges secure against strong winter gales.
**You:** How do you find good tiles among the ruins?
**Ghost:** Nettles and thick briars cover the fallen heaps of ancient stone near the walls. My husband clears away brambles with a billhook to reach the sound red tiles buried underneath. Uncovering flat pieces takes patience, but they make sturdy stones for patching our hearth.
**You:** What equipment helps you farm?
**Ghost:** Good tide to you. Mind the mud in the furrows as you go.

**You:** How is your barley bread baked?
**Ghost:** Grinding grain between heavy stones yields coarse meal for our daily loaves. The bread turns out dark brown with a tough crust that keeps out dampness. We slice it thick to soak up bean broth, giving my family plenty of strength for working in the fields.

**Ghost:** Walking to the nearby town market allows us to trade extra garden produce for household goods. The market square bustling with noise, livestock, and pedlars is a grand sight. I always return home tired, but glad of the salt or thread I traded for.
