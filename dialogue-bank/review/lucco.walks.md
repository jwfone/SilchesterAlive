# lucco walks

### Walk 1

**Ghost:** Hold there a moment. I am Lucco, rider for a great house here. We have a fine timber home inside the dykes. Tell me, have you come far to our town?
**You:** Where do you live in the town?
**Ghost:** Home for me is a timber-built dwelling set just behind the old alignments. With a few compact rooms for sleeping and storage, it gives my family all the shelter we need. The thick clay walls keep out the bitter winds that blow across the open fields.
**You:** Tell me about the tracks you ride on.
**Ghost:** Worn dirt tracks and narrow lanes criss-cross our town, keeping to the ancient ways. They are dusty in the heat of summer and turn to deep mud when the winter rains fall. My horse knows every bend and turn by heart.
**You:** Do you use coins to buy food?
**Ghost:** Local traders exchange silver coins, iron goods, and farm produce in the open air near our lanes. I sometimes carry silver coins stamped with the king's name, though many folk still prefer to swap grain for iron. It is a busy place when the harvest comes in.
**You:** Where does the food come from?
**Ghost:** Fields beyond the dykes grow grain, while cattle and sheep graze nearby pastures. Farmers work hard to fill the granaries of our leading households. I often ride past crops of barley and spelt wheat waving in the breeze, with sturdy cattle chewing grass nearby.
**You:** Why does the town have huge banks of soil?
**Ghost:** Our people dug deep ditches and piled the earth high to make these protective dykes. They have stood since I was a boy, a reminder of the strength of our tribe. They keep the cold winds out and unwanted visitors away.
**You:** How is this settlement arranged?
**Ghost:** Farewell. I must feed my mount now. Keep safe inside the dykes.

### Walk 2

**Ghost:** Back again, are you? It is good to see a friendly face. My horse and I have just finished our rounds. What more would you like to know of our town?
**You:** What is your job in this town?
**Ghost:** Folk call me Lucco, and I serve as a trusted rider for one of the main houses inside our earthwork banks. It is my duty to carry spoken words and sealed tokens between households across our territory, keeping our people connected day and night.
**You:** Tell me about the earthwork banks.
**Ghost:** Our people dug deep ditches and piled the earth high to make these protective dykes. They have stood since I was a boy, a reminder of the strength of our tribe. They keep the cold winds out and unwanted visitors away.
**You:** What is the weather like?
**Ghost:** Summer is the best time for a rider, when the ground is firm and we can gallop. When winter comes, the mist hangs low over the dykes and the paths turn to deep puddles. My horse knows how to find her footing even when the snow falls.
**You:** Do the tracks get dusty in hot weather?
**Ghost:** Hot summer daylight lasts long, letting me journey across the district from dawn until dusk. Dry paths let us fly, but hard-baked soil jars the horse's legs if I push too hard. I keep her to a steady rhythm, pausing often so she can graze green grass in the cool shade.
**You:** How do you ride through winter mud?
**Ghost:** Thick slush clings to the mare's fetlocks, so I walk her past the deepest puddles. Patience keeps us upright when the earthen banks turn slick. If the sludge reaches above her hooves, I dismount, guiding her gently along the grassy verges where the turf holds firmer beneath our boots.
**You:** Tell me about your horse.
**Ghost:** Farewell. I must feed my mount now. Keep safe inside the dykes.

### Walk 3

**Ghost:** You return. I was just checking the girth on my mount. There is always plenty to talk about here within the safety of the king's great earthworks. Ask away.
**You:** How do you spend your daily routine?
**Ghost:** My day begins at first light by checking my horse's hooves, brushing down his coat, and offering fresh water. After eating a quick bowl of warm pottage, I report to the household to receive messages and spend the remaining daylight hours riding along our town lanes.
**You:** Who is the king here?
**Ghost:** Folk say our king stayed friends with Rome, though he does not sleep in our house. My master sends me riding to deliver messages for the household, but we do not see the great ruler himself. He keeps his own grand quarters elsewhere, far from our simple timber walls.
**You:** What is happening on the edge of town?
**Ghost:** Fresh digging has started near the edge of our tracks, where the ground rises. The workers are busy with spades, and I can see new roofs catching the sun. It is a noisy place now, full of shouting builders and heavy carts.
**You:** What does the town of Calleva look like?
**Ghost:** Calleva is an ancient and bustling settlement framed by high defensive banks. Our lanes bend naturally through the landscape, linking timber compounds, open market areas, and smithies. It is a lively place where local Atrebates gather to trade goods, share news, and conduct tribal business.
**You:** Tell me about the tracks you ride on.
**Ghost:** Our lanes still follow the old alignments that our fathers trod before the Romans came. They wind between the timber houses and the small garden plots, busy with dogs and chickens. You must ride slowly to avoid bumping into folk.
**You:** Do wheels get stuck in the mud?
**Ghost:** Safe travels on the lanes. May your horse be swift.

### Walk 4

**Ghost:** You return. I was just checking the girth on my mount. There is always plenty to talk about here within the safety of the king's great earthworks. Ask away.
**You:** Do you know the king?
**Ghost:** Our king rules these lands with the backing of Rome, as far as I know. He does not sleep under our roof, for our home is just a timber cottage. I only ride the lanes carrying messages that might one day reach his ears.
**You:** What do you remember from when you were little?
**Ghost:** When the legions arrived, I was only a lad helper tending to the ponies. We heard tales of great battles elsewhere, but our leaders chose to make peace instead. Since that time, our lanes have grown busier with new kinds of trade.
**You:** What are you wearing?
**Ghost:** A heavy wool cloak is my best shield against the cold British drizzle as I gallop along the tracks. Underneath, I wear a soft linen shirt and thick woollen trousers tucked into soft leather shoes. It keeps me warm whether riding or resting.
**You:** Who lives in your house?
**Ghost:** My family share the few rooms of our timber house, from the youngest children to the elders. Everyone has a task, whether it is spinning wool, cooking pottage, or helping me check the horse's leather tack. We find comfort in being crowded together when winter winds howl.
**You:** What food do you have?
**Ghost:** Simple fare fills my wooden bowl. Mostly I eat thick grain pottage sweetened with a little wild honey, or some salted pork if the hunt went well. On the trail, a hard crust of rye bread is my constant companion, keeping my energy up as my horse eats sweet grass.
**You:** How do you cook your food?
**Ghost:** Safe travels on the lanes. May your horse be swift.

**Ghost:** Simple iron tools are what we use, and my belt knife is my constant companion. I use it to shave down wood, clean out my horse's frogs, or peel wild apples in the woods. It is a practical tool, made of good local iron that holds its edge.

**Ghost:** Dawn usually finds me out by the stable, inspecting tack and making sure my mount is sound. Once my morning duties for the household are assigned, I take to the road. Evening brings me back inside the dykes to share food and rest by the hearth.

**Ghost:** Countless builders worked together, swinging antler tools to break through rock and stubborn clay. The ditch outside was dug deep so attackers would have to scramble upwards. Piled dirt formed the rampart, creating a barrier that still defies both winter washouts and creeping roots.

**You:** What keeps the wind out of your home?
**Ghost:** Hazel wattles woven between upright timber posts give our house its strength. We plaster these woven hurdles with clay, water, and chopped straw until no cracks remain.
